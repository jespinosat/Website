Marketing Picked the Market. Which Estimator Should You Trust?¶

Many of the most expensive decisions a consumer app makes, like marketing campaigns, regional launches, or pricing changes, happen at the market level, where randomizing individual users is impossible. For a family safety app, a back-to-school campaign is a natural example: it runs in one metro, it lands during the season when parents are most likely to subscribe anyway, and the metro was not chosen at random. Sometimes it is the company's biggest market, and sometimes it is the market that already looked the most promising.

This notebook simulates both versions of that decision with a known true effect and compares two standard ways of measuring it. Difference-in-differences compares the treated metro to the average of all other metros, while synthetic control builds a custom comparison out of the metros that most closely track the treated one before the campaign. The takeaway is that neither method wins everywhere. When marketing picks a large market that grows like everyone else, DiD recovers the true effect and synthetic control is biased, because matching an unusually high baseline forces it to lean on a few big metros that behave differently. When marketing picks a market because it was already growing fast, the result flips: DiD credits the campaign with growth and seasonality that would have happened anyway, and synthetic control recovers the true effect. The real lesson is to check which assumption your data can support before choosing an estimator.

1. Setup: Did the Back-to-School Campaign Drive Premium Subscriptions?¶

A family safety app with a free tier and a paid premium membership runs a regional back-to-school marketing campaign (TV, streaming, and billboards) in one metro area, promoting its premium features to parents. Leadership wants to know how many incremental paid subscriptions the campaign generated, to decide whether to roll it out nationally. The campaign runs at the market level, so there is no user-level randomization: every household in the metro is exposed, and no household outside it is. The only option is to compare the treated metro against the other metros that did not get the campaign.

The comparison gets tricky in a different way depending on how marketing picked the metro, so we simulate two scenarios:

  • Scenario A: a top market. Marketing launches in one of its largest metros. Baseline subscriptions are much higher than in the typical metro, but organic growth and back-to-school sensitivity are average. Parallel trends holds against the full set of controls.
  • Scenario B: a hand-picked growth market. Marketing chooses a mid-size metro because premium adoption was already growing fast there, and it reacts more strongly than average to the back-to-school spike, which the campaign launches right before. Parallel trends does not hold.

Difference-in-differences compares the treated metro to the average control metro after removing each metro's baseline level. A high baseline does not matter to it, but a steeper trend or a stronger seasonal spike leaks straight into its estimate. Synthetic control has the opposite profile. It builds a weighted combination of control metros that matches the treated metro's pre-campaign path, level included, which means it can absorb faster growth and stronger seasonality, as long as some control metros look similar enough to combine into a match. When the treated metro sits near the top of the level distribution, only a handful of controls can get there, and their growth and seasonality come along with them.

2. Data Generating Process¶

We observe $J + 1$ metro areas over $T$ weeks. Metro $j = 0$ receives the campaign starting in week $T_0$; metros $j = 1, \dots, J$ never do. The outcome $Y_{jt}$ is weekly new premium subscriptions per 100,000 households.

Untreated potential outcome. Absent the campaign, subscriptions follow a factor model:

$$ Y_{jt}(0) = \alpha_j + \delta_t + \gamma_j \, t + \lambda_j \, f_t + \varepsilon_{jt}, \qquad \varepsilon_{jt} \sim N(0, \sigma^2) $$

where:

  • $\alpha_j \sim N(\mu_\alpha, \sigma_\alpha^2)$ is the metro's baseline subscription level (market size, awareness, income).
  • $\delta_t$ is a national shock common to all metros (app store features, national news), drawn as a random walk: $\delta_t = \delta_{t-1} + \nu_t$, $\nu_t \sim N(0, \sigma_\nu^2)$.
  • $\gamma_j \sim U(\gamma_{min}, \gamma_{max})$ is the metro's organic growth rate in premium adoption.
  • $f_t$ is the back-to-school seasonal factor, shared by all metros, peaking at the week school starts ($w^*$ in week-of-year terms, with spread $s$):

$$ f_t = \exp\left(-\frac{(w(t) - w^*)^2}{2 s^2}\right), \qquad w(t) = \big((t - 1) \bmod 52\big) + 1 $$

  • $\lambda_j \sim U(\lambda_{min}, \lambda_{max})$ is the metro's sensitivity to the back-to-school season.

The treated metro. Rather than drawing at random, the treated metro's parameters are set to reflect how marketing picked it. Both scenarios share the same control metros, shocks, and noise; only metro $0$ changes:

$$ \text{Scenario A (top market):} \quad \alpha_0 \gg \bar{\alpha}_{\text{controls}}, \qquad \gamma_0 = \bar{\gamma}_{\text{controls}}, \qquad \lambda_0 = \bar{\lambda}_{\text{controls}} $$

$$ \text{Scenario B (growth market):} \quad \gamma_0 > \bar{\gamma}_{\text{controls}}, \qquad \lambda_0 > \bar{\lambda}_{\text{controls}} $$

In both scenarios, every parameter of the treated metro stays inside the range of the control metros, so in principle a weighted combination of controls can reproduce its path. Scenario A places $\alpha_0$ near the top of that range, where very few controls can help.

Treatment. The campaign adds a constant effect $\tau$ from week $T_0$ onward:

$$ Y_{jt} = Y_{jt}(0) + \tau \cdot D_{jt}, \qquad D_{jt} = \mathbb{1}[j = 0] \cdot \mathbb{1}[t \geq T_0] $$

Why DiD fails in Scenario B. DiD recovers $\tau$ only if the treated metro's untreated path would have moved in parallel with the control average. The metro fixed effect absorbs any difference in $\alpha$, so the bias depends only on growth and seasonality:

$$ \text{Bias}_{DiD} \approx \underbrace{(\gamma_0 - \bar{\gamma}_{co})(\bar{t}_{post} - \bar{t}_{pre})}_{\text{faster growth}} + \underbrace{(\lambda_0 - \bar{\lambda}_{co})(\bar{f}_{post} - \bar{f}_{pre})}_{\text{stronger seasonal spike}} $$

In Scenario A both terms are zero, so DiD is unbiased no matter how large the metro is. In Scenario B both terms are positive: the treated metro was growing faster and the post period contains the seasonal peak, so DiD credits the campaign with growth and seasonality that would have happened anyway.

Why synthetic control fails in Scenario A. Synthetic control has no intercept: its counterfactual is a weighted average of control outcomes, with weights $w_j \geq 0$ that sum to one. Its bias is whatever gap in level, growth, and seasonality the weights fail to close:

$$ \text{Bias}_{SC} \approx \underbrace{\Big(\alpha_0 - \sum_j w_j \alpha_j\Big)}_{\text{level gap}} + \underbrace{\Big(\gamma_0 - \sum_j w_j \gamma_j\Big)\bar{t}_{post}}_{\text{growth gap}} + \underbrace{\Big(\lambda_0 - \sum_j w_j \lambda_j\Big)\bar{f}_{post}}_{\text{seasonal gap}} $$

In Scenario B the treated metro has a typical level, so the weights are free to match its growth and seasonality, which drives all three terms toward zero. In Scenario A, reaching a baseline near the top of the distribution forces the weights onto the few largest control metros, and their growth rates and seasonal sensitivities come along whether they match or not. The pre-period fit can still look reasonable, but the growth and seasonal gaps compound after the launch, right as the back-to-school peak arrives.

np.random.seed(2026)
J = 41 #number of Cities (lets make the first city the treated one)

T_0 = 82 #campaign starts ~2 weeks before year-two school start

#Simulate metro level variables
mu_alpha      = 50 #baseline mean of subscriptions
sigma_alpha   = 10
#organic weekly growth
gamma_jlow , gamma_jhigh  = 0,0.15 
gamma0        = 0.11 #treated metro growth, above the control mean of ~0.075
#seasonal sensitivity
lambda_jlow,lambda_jhigh   = 5,15
lambda0       = 13 #treated metro sensitivity, above the control mean of ~10

alpha0_benign = 70          #benign launch metro is a top market (controls max ~77)


metro_level_data = pd.DataFrame(
    {
        "metro_id": np.arange(1,J+1),
        "D_j": np.concatenate(([1], np.repeat(0, J-1))),
        "alpha_j":np.random.normal(loc=mu_alpha,scale=sigma_alpha,size=J),
        "gamma_j":np.random.uniform(gamma_jlow,gamma_jhigh,J),
        "lambda_j":np.random.uniform(lambda_jlow,lambda_jhigh,J)
    }
)


#Set Seasonality and organic growth for treated city
metro_level_data.loc[0,'gamma_j'] = gamma0
metro_level_data.loc[0,'lambda_j'] = lambda0

# calculate means for the benign case
metro_level_data_controls = metro_level_data.iloc[1:]

mean_params = metro_level_data_controls[['gamma_j','lambda_j']].mean()

metro_level_data['gamma_j_benign'] = np.where(metro_level_data['D_j']==1, mean_params['gamma_j'],metro_level_data['gamma_j'])
metro_level_data['lambda_j_benign'] = np.where(metro_level_data['D_j']==1, mean_params['lambda_j'],metro_level_data['lambda_j']) 
metro_level_data['alpha_j_benign'] = np.where(metro_level_data['D_j']==1, alpha0_benign, metro_level_data['alpha_j'])   

#Simulate time variables
sigma_v  = 0.3 #national shock volatility
delta0   = 0
delta    = np.zeros(T)
delta[0] = delta0

#school starts around week 32 (early August), spike lasts a few weeks
w_star, s =32, 3
f0       = np.exp(- (1-w_star)**2/(2*s**2))
f = np.zeros(T)
f[0]     = f0
for t in range(1,len(delta)):
  delta[t] = delta[t-1] + np.random.normal(loc=0,scale=sigma_v )
  wt     = t%52+1
  f[t]   = np.exp( -(wt-w_star)**2/(2*s**2))

time_variables = pd.DataFrame({
  "t": np.arange(1,T+1),
  "delta_t":delta,
  "f_t": f
}) 

#Now simulate panel data
sigma    = 2 #weekly noise
panel_data = pd.DataFrame({"metro_id":  np.repeat(np.arange(1,J+1), T) ,
                         "e_jt":np.random.normal(loc=0,scale=sigma,size=J*T)})

panel_data['t'] = panel_data.groupby(['metro_id']).cumcount() +1 # generate the weekly period variable

 
experiment_data = pd.merge(panel_data,metro_level_data, on= 'metro_id', how='left').merge(time_variables,how='left',on='t')
experiment_data

2. Estimate Models and Compare¶

Now, let's estimate the models for both methods and different processes. As the results below show, under the benign scenario, the estimates are close, but when seasonality is introduced and the chosen treated city was growing more already, we can see that the Dif-in-Dif method overestimates the results, while the synthetic control one seems to do a better job at estimating the effect.

    time_optimize_ssr=pre_weeks,
    unit_variable="metro_id",
    time_variable="t",
